Overdrive Asset Production · Lesson 10

Animation, cutscene assets, and presentation separation

2026-08-09 · Mission: MISSION.md · ~60 min

The question this lesson answers

How do you keep runtime vehicle animation, driver animation, validation clips, and cutscene presentation from becoming one tangled asset?

An animation file is not automatically production content. First identify who owns the motion, why it exists, and where it is allowed to run.

Lesson 6 introduced action classification during export validation. This lesson applies that distinction to production structure: the car and driver remain separate asset domains, procedural vehicle motion remains separate from imported clips, and cinematic presentation remains separate from simulation-owned objects.

1. Four kinds of animation content

CategoryPurposeExampleProduction treatment
Runtime car presentationResponds continuously to vehicle stateWheel rolling, steering, suspension, dashboardOwn by car visual controllers or deliberately mapped runtime clips
Runtime driver presentationResponds to steering and gear stateDriver torso, arms, hands, fingersOwn by a reusable DriverPrefab and driver controller
Validation actionProves alignment, pose, or pivot behaviorVAL_* neutral or inspection actionsUse temporarily, then remove from production assignments
Cutscene presentationServes a cinematic camera or scripted momentIntro pose, finish presentation, pit service shotKeep in a presentation layer or dedicated cutscene asset

The same transform channel can be technically valid in all four categories while still being wrong for a particular owner. A steering action applied to the car mesh is not automatically a valid driver action, and a validation pose is not automatically a cutscene animation.

2. The car and driver are separate asset domains

The production boundary is:

CarPrefab
├── Cockpit
├── SteeringWheel
├── Dashboard
├── Wheels
├── Suspension
└── Vehicle lights

DriverPrefab
├── Driver mesh
├── Driver rig
├── Arms and hands
├── Helmet
└── Clothing

The car owns the steering wheel, dashboard, wheels, suspension, and vehicle lights. The driver owns the body, arms, hands, helmet, and clothing. A source package may place both in one Blender scene for convenience, but that does not make them one production asset.

The Benetton inventory confirms this boundary: the car contains STEER_HR, wheel roots, suspension groups, and dashboard candidates, while no DRIVER:* rig is embedded in the car FBX. The driver is an external reusable asset.

3. Decide whether motion is procedural or imported

Imported animation is not automatically better than procedural animation. Choose ownership according to the visual feature.

FeaturePreferred ownershipReason
Wheel rollingProcedural car controllerDepends continuously on vehicle speed and wheel radius
Front-wheel steeringProcedural car controllerMust match the current steering state every frame
Suspension travelProcedural or mapped car presentationDepends on per-corner compression and needs pivot validation
Steering wheel rotationProcedural car controllerMust remain synchronized with the vehicle steering angle
Dashboard readoutsTelemetry-driven presentationSpeed, RPM, gear, and fuel are live values, not fixed clips
Driver steering poseDriver animation/controllerUses steering state but affects the separate driver rig
Gear-change gestureImported driver clip or driver state machineDiscrete presentation event with a clear start and end
Rain helmet effectWeather/presentation systemIt is an environmental effect, not car animation

The Benetton inventory shows why blind conversion fails: suspension clips contain wheel and tire target names, but the measured wheel transforms do not independently change in the sample. The clip contains target names; it does not prove that every target needs an independent runtime animation.

4. Validation actions must leave production bones

Validation actions are diagnostic tools. They may place a body, hand, wheel, or suspension assembly in a useful inspection pose, but their assignment must not remain after the check.

Validation action
  → assign temporarily
  → inspect alignment or clipping
  → capture evidence
  → restore neutral pose
  → remove validation assignment

Actions with the VAL_ prefix belong to this category unless a production decision explicitly promotes one. Leaving them assigned can create clipping, rotation, or pose changes that look like runtime bugs but are actually stale validation state.

5. Driver fitting has an order

Driver animation cannot be trusted until the body fits the cockpit. Use this order:

  1. Place the driver root in the seat.
  2. Fit pelvis and torso to the seat and cockpit clearance.
  3. Fit head, helmet, legs, and feet.
  4. Verify that the car is fully visible and the driver is not intersecting the cockpit.
  5. Fit steering-wheel center, depth, tilt, and radius.
  6. Only then validate hands, forearms, and gear-change poses.

Animating hands before the body and steering-wheel relationship is stable creates a false result. If the seat or torso moves later, the hand animation must be redone.

6. Why “export all actions” is not a production plan

The Ferrari learning export enabled bake_anim_use_all_actions=True and produced 154 Unity AnimationClip subassets from 10 source actions and 283 objects. That proves baking occurred. It does not prove that 154 clips are wanted.

Before export, write the intentional list:

Car production actions:
  [explicit list]

Driver production actions:
  [explicit list]

Validation actions:
  [explicit list; excluded from production]

Cutscene actions:
  [explicit list; owned by presentation]

For the Benetton pilot, external clips such as steer.ksanim, shift_up.ksanim, and shift_dw.ksanim target a driver rig or a specific car presentation domain. They require a mapping layer; blind name matching is not a valid import strategy.

7. Cutscene presentation is not the production car

A cutscene may need a special pose, camera-facing steering angle, wheel blur, pit-service configuration, or temporary visibility state. Those requirements should not mutate the canonical gameplay car hierarchy.

Gameplay assetPresentation asset or layer
Canonical car mesh and runtime hierarchyCutscene duplicate or presentation root
Simulation-driven steering and wheel stateScripted cinematic pose
Reusable DriverPrefabCutscene-specific driver pose or animation track
Runtime cameraCinematic camera and shot framing
Gameplay VFX and audio eventsPresentation timing, emphasis, and shot-specific effects

The purpose is not to forbid reuse. It is to make the ownership explicit so a cutscene adjustment cannot silently change the gameplay asset.

8. Controlled exercise

  1. Open the Ferrari or Benetton learning asset and save a working copy.
  2. Separate the car hierarchy from the driver hierarchy in the scene or export plan.
  3. List the car-owned visual domains: wheels, suspension, steering wheel, dashboard, and lights.
  4. List the driver-owned domains: body, arms, hands, helmet, and clothing.
  5. Classify every available action as Production, Validation, Duplicate, Unrelated, or Cutscene.
  6. Assign one validation action temporarily and capture the alignment evidence.
  7. Restore the neutral pose and remove the validation assignment.
  8. Fit the driver body before testing the steering and gear-change poses.
  9. Write the intentional production action list before exporting.
  10. Record which presentation changes must remain outside the canonical gameplay hierarchy.
Expected reasoning

A successful result is not “the animation plays.” It is: each motion has an owner, car and driver boundaries are preserved, validation actions do not remain assigned, the driver is fitted before hand animation, and cutscene presentation does not silently mutate the gameplay asset.

Decision rule

identify the moving domain
  → assign the owner
  → classify the action
  → fit static relationships first
  → validate temporarily
  → remove diagnostic state
  → export only intentional content
  → keep cutscene presentation separate
